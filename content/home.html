✅ The Checklist

    Why Keep a National Park Checklist?

    There are 63 national parks in the United States, scattered from the Arctic tundra of Alaska to the coral reefs of the Virgin Islands, and keeping track of which ones you have actually set foot in is harder than it sounds. Most travelers can name the famous ones they have visited — Yellowstone, Yosemite, the Grand Canyon — but the count gets fuzzy somewhere around park number twelve. A national park checklist turns a vague memory into a concrete record: every park you have visited, checked off, timestamped, and counted toward a goal you can see growing.

    The idea of "collecting" the parks is not new. For decades, visitors have carried the official Passport To Your National Parks book, collecting ink cancellation stamps at visitor centers across the country. Online checklists are the digital twin of that tradition: faster to update, impossible to lose, and shareable with friends and family. Whether you are planning a decade-long quest to see all 63 or simply want to remember which parks the kids have already earned their Junior Ranger badges in, a checklist is the foundation.

    The 63 Parks, From Acadia to Zion

    The checklist covers every park currently designated as a U.S. national park, alphabetically from Acadia's rocky Maine coastline to Zion's towering Utah canyon. The list spans obvious icons and quiet surprises alike. Alaska alone holds eight of them, several reachable only by bush plane or boat, which is why most collectors save the Alaska parks for dedicated trips. Utah's Mighty 5 — Arches, Bryce Canyon, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, and Zion — make the most efficient cluster for checking off several parks in one road trip. California's nine parks reward repeat visits across deserts, mountains, and coastline.

    Each park on the list links to its own page on this site, with highlights of what to see, what to check off while you are there, and which other parks share its state so you can plan efficient multi-park routes.     Tips for Checking Off More Parks

    Cluster by region. The single biggest accelerator is geography: one well-planned road trip through southern Utah can check off five parks in a week, and a Florida loop covers Biscayne, Everglades, and Dry Tortugas. Visit in shoulder seasons. Spring and fall mean thinner crowds, easier lodging, and open roads at most parks — plus the checklist does not care what month the stamp says. Count it only if you were there. Collectors' honor: driving through without stopping does not count, but even a short visitor-center stop does. Bring the kids into it. The Junior Ranger program gives children a booklet, activities, and a badge at nearly every park — it turns a checklist into a family tradition. Record the date. This checklist timestamps every check-off automatically, so years from now you will know not just that you went, but when.

    National Park Checklist FAQ

    How many U.S. national parks are there?

    There are 63 national parks in the United States as designated by Congress and managed by the National Park Service. The count has grown over time — New River Gorge in West Virginia became the 63rd in late 2020 — and this checklist tracks all 63.

    Is this national park checklist free?

    Yes, completely free. There is no account, no signup, no app to install, and no subscription. Your check-offs, levels, badges, and streaks are stored in your browser's local storage on your own device.

    Will my progress be saved if I close the browser?

    Yes. Everything is saved to local storage in your browser, so your checked-off parks persist between visits on the same device and browser. Note that clearing browser data or switching devices will reset the local copy — the share card is a good way to snapshot milestones.

    What counts as "visiting" a national park?

    Collectors' honor applies: most people count a park if they entered it and spent time there — even a visitor-center stop counts. Driving past on the highway without stopping generally does not. Whatever rule you choose, apply it consistently.

    What is the difference between a checklist, a tracker, and a passport book?

    A checklist is the simple list you tick off; a tracker adds statistics, levels, and history over time; the passport book is the official physical booklet sold by park partners where you collect ink cancellation stamps at visitor centers. This site offers the digital checklist and tracker for free, and our guides explain the physical passport book and stamps.

    Which national parks are the hardest to check off?

    The eight Alaska parks are the toughest for most travelers — several, like Gates of the Arctic and Kobuk Valley, have no roads and require air taxis. American Samoa and the Virgin Islands require dedicated flights, and Isle Royale in Lake Superior is reachable only by ferry or seaplane with a short season.

    Can I share my progress with friends?

    Yes. Use the "Generate my share card" button in the stats panel to create a downloadable image showing your park count, level, achievements, and streak. It is watermarked with this site's name and sized for social media.

    Do I need the official passport book too?

    Not required, but many collectors use both: this site for the live count and stats, the physical book for ink stamps as souvenirs. See our passport book guide for editions and where to buy one.
