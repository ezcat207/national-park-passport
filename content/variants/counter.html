    "So How Many Have You Been To?"

    Every national park enthusiast knows the question. It comes up at dinner parties, at trailheads, in the comments of travel photos: how many of the 63 have you visited? It is a simple question that most people cannot answer accurately. They guess. They say "around fifteen" or "maybe twenty." The counter exists to replace the guess with a number you can defend — because you built it one tap at a time, park by park.

    The number matters more than it seems. It is your score in the longest-running game in American travel: the quest to see all 63 national parks. It is a conversation starter, a trip-planning input, and a personal record all at once. And unlike most scores in life, this one only goes up.

    Why Counting Is Harder Than It Sounds

    Counting parks sounds trivial until you try it from memory. The human brain is bad at exhaustive recall of a 63-item list. You will remember the spectacular ones — the Grand Canyon at sunset, Old Faithful erupting — and forget the quiet ones. Did that 2014 road trip include Great Basin, or did you just drive past the turnoff? Was the stop in Ohio a national park or a state park? (Cuyahoga Valley counts; most Ohio state parks do not.)

    Then there are the definitional questions. The National Park Service manages more than 430 units, but only 63 carry the exact title "National Park." National monuments, historic sites, and seashores are wonderful, but they do not increment this counter. Purists count only the 63 — and this counter enforces that discipline by listing exactly those 63, no more, no less.

    The reliable method is systematic: walk the full list once, decide park by park, and let the counter do the arithmetic. Most first-time counters discover their real number differs from their guess — usually higher, because forgotten trips surface as they scan the names.

    Milestones Worth Counting Down To

    Every counter needs milestones, and the 63-park quest has natural ones. 1 — the first park, where every collector starts. 10 — double digits, the point where "I like parks" becomes "I'm collecting them." 21 — one-third of the way. 32 — the halfway mark, a genuine achievement most travelers never reach. 50 — the home stretch, fewer than a quarter remaining. 63 — the complete set, a feat accomplished by a small, dedicated fraternity of travelers.

    This site's achievement system marks 1, 10, 25, 50, and 63 automatically, and the level ladder — Explorer, Adventurer, Ranger, Legend — gives the count a title to wear. When the counter hits a milestone, the share card on the main dashboard turns it into an image worth posting.

    The Most Common Counts (and What They Mean)

    Talk to enough collectors and patterns emerge. 3–8 parks: the casual enthusiast — usually the famous western parks plus one near home. The quest has not started; these are just great trips. 9–20: the awakening — somewhere in here, most collectors buy the passport book or start a list, and trips start getting planned around parks. 21–40: the committed collector — regional clusters are being knocked out, Alaska is being schemed about. 41–55: the serious quest — remaining parks are mostly the hard ones: remote Alaska units, American Samoa, Isle Royale. 56–63: the endgame — every trip is targeted, and the final park often gets a ceremonial visit.

    Wherever your number lands, the counter's job is the same: an honest number, and a clear view of what comes next.

    Counting Rules Collectors Actually Use

    The community has converged on sensible norms. Presence counts, not depth. You do not need to hike every trail; entering the park and experiencing it counts. A visitor-center stop counts. If you went in, you were there. Drive-throughs are debated. Most collectors require stopping — wheels stopped, feet on the ground. Childhood visits count. Being carried through Yellowstone at age four still counts; you were there. Revisits do not double-count. The counter measures distinct parks, not total visits — though your memories will treasure the repeats. Be consistent. Whatever rules you adopt, apply them to all 63, past and future.

    Growing Your Number Faster

    Want the number to climb? Geography is the lever. Cluster trips knock out 3–5 parks per vacation: Utah's Mighty 5, the California desert trio (Death Valley, Joshua Tree, Channel Islands as a stretch goal), Florida's three, the Pacific Northwest three. Gateway cities turn layovers into counts: St. Louis (Gateway Arch), Tucson (Saguaro), Cleveland/Akron (Cuyahoga Valley), Little Rock (Hot Springs). Shoulder seasons stretch the budget further with cheaper lodging. And start the Alaska fund now — eight parks, most fly-in only, and the earlier you start saving, the sooner the number jumps.

    The Counter as a Conversation Starter

    Here is an underrated use of the counter: it makes you interesting at parties. "I've been to 31 national parks" lands differently from "I like hiking" — it invites questions, trading of tips, and the occasional friendly rivalry. Collectors report that stating the number out loud also creates accountability: once friends know your count, skipping a planned trip feels like letting the number down. Some families post the count on the fridge and update it with a marker after every trip, turning the number into a household tradition. Whatever motivates you — competition, curiosity, or the quiet satisfaction of watching a number grow — the counter gives the quest a scoreboard. And scoreboards, as any sports fan knows, make the game worth playing.

    National Park Counter FAQ

    How many national parks have I visited?

    Tap each park you've been to in the counter above — your total is computed automatically. For the headline number with your level and badges, see the main checklist dashboard.

    Which parks count toward the 63?

    Only units officially designated "National Park" — exactly the 63 on this list. National monuments, historic parks, and seashores are managed by the same agency but do not count toward the 63.

    Does visiting the same park twice count twice?

    No — the counter tracks distinct parks visited, out of 63. Repeat visits are wonderful but do not change the number.

    What is a good number of parks to have visited?

    Any number is good — but collectors often treat 10 as the start of the quest, 32 (halfway) as a major milestone, and 50+ as the serious endgame. The U.S. average is far lower than most enthusiasts guess.

    Is the counter free?

    Yes, free with no account or signup. Your count is saved in your browser.

    Can I share my count?
