    What Makes a Park Map "Interactive"?

    A static map shows you where the parks are. An interactive map lets you ask questions of the geography: show me only the parks in Utah; show me which Alaska parks I have left; find every park with "canyon" in the name. The filters above are the interactive layer — each one re-renders the atlas around your question. It is the difference between looking at a poster and interrogating a dataset.

    Interactivity also means the map responds to you. Tapping a park marks it visited, and the atlas remembers — the same data powers your checklist, your level, and your badges on the main dashboard. An interactive map is not just a reference; it is a living record of your quest, filtered however you need to see it today.

    Touring the 63 by Region

    The West is the heartland. The great majority of the 63 lie west of the Rockies, and any interactive exploration should start here. Alaska's eight are the wild frontier — Denali, Glacier Bay, Katmai, Kenai Fjords, Kobuk Valley, Lake Clark, Wrangell-St. Elias, and Gates of the Arctic — most requiring planes, boats, or serious expedition planning. The Southwest cluster (Arizona, Utah, Nevada, New Mexico, Colorado) is the densest concentration of iconic parks on Earth: Grand Canyon, Zion, Arches, Bryce Canyon, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Mesa Verde, Great Sand Dunes, Black Canyon of the Gunnison, Rocky Mountain, Saguaro, Petrified Forest, Carlsbad Caverns, White Sands, Guadalupe Mountains. California's nine span Death Valley's extremes, Joshua Tree's deserts, the Sierra giants (Yosemite, Sequoia, Kings Canyon), the volcanic north (Lassen), the redwood coast, Pinnacles, and the Channel Islands offshore. The Pacific Northwest offers Olympic, Mount Rainier, North Cascades, and Crater Lake.

    The middle of the country holds the prairie and plains parks: Badlands, Wind Cave, Theodore Roosevelt, Voyageurs, Isle Royale, Indiana Dunes, Cuyahoga Valley, Gateway Arch, Hot Springs, Mammoth Cave. The South and East hold the Smokies (the most-visited park of all), Shenandoah, Congaree, New River Gorge, plus Florida's trio of Everglades, Biscayne, and Dry Tortugas. The far-flung — Hawaii's Haleakala and Hawaii Volcanoes, American Samoa, the Virgin Islands — are the expedition tier. Filter the explorer above by state to tour each region park by park.

    Using the Interactive Map to Plan Trips

    The trip-planning workflow with an interactive map has four steps. 1. Filter to your target region. Driving the Southwest? Filter UT, AZ, NV, CO and see every candidate. 2. Subtract the visited. Your tapped parks are struck through — what remains is the trip. 3. Check the gaps. Sort by state to see which states are one park away from completion; finishing a state earns a badge. 4. Sequence the route. With the candidate list in front of you, the driving order usually suggests itself — the trip planner page walks through building the actual itinerary.

    Two planning truths the map teaches: first, density beats distance — a week in southern Utah yields five parks, while a week chasing singletons yields one. Second, the map lies about Alaska — those eight pins look close together on a small map and are separated by hundreds of roadless miles. Budget and plan accordingly.

    Interactive Map vs. Paper Map vs. Scratch-Off Poster

    Each has a job. The interactive map (this page) is for planning: filtering, searching, and linking each park to its detail page. The paper road atlas is for the car: no battery, no signal required, and the big picture that screens never quite deliver. The scratch-off poster is for the wall: the trophy display that turns 63 foil rectangles into a conversation piece. Serious collectors use all three — the interactive map to plan, the atlas to navigate, the poster to commemorate.

    Park Geography Trivia Worth Knowing

    Exploring the map surfaces delightful facts. The northernmost park is Gates of the Arctic, above the Arctic Circle; the southernmost is American Samoa, south of the equator — the only park in the Southern Hemisphere. The largest is Wrangell-St. Elias in Alaska, bigger than several countries; the smallest is Gateway Arch in St. Louis, an urban park. The most-visited is Great Smoky Mountains; the least-visited are the roadless Alaska parks and Isle Royale. Three parks span state lines in the data above (Death Valley, Great Smoky Mountains, Yellowstone) — filter by either state to find them. And the "Mighty 5" of Utah fit inside an area smaller than many single eastern states, which is why the Utah loop is the classic first multi-park expedition.

    From Exploration to Action

    An interactive map is only as good as the trips it inspires, so build a habit around it. Once a month, spend ten minutes with the explorer: filter to a region you have been neglecting, note the unvisited parks, and read one park detail page you have never opened. Curiosity compounds — the park you idly read about in March becomes the detour you take in July. Pair the habit with the tracker: exploration finds the candidates, the tracker records the conquests. And when the list of "someday" parks starts feeling long, remember that every collector with a full map once stared at the same 63 names and wondered where to start. They started with one filter, one state, one trip. So can you.

    Interactive National Park Map FAQ

    How do I use the interactive map?

    Use the search box to find parks by name, the state dropdown to filter to one state (like a map layer), and the sort control to group by state. Tap any park to mark it visited — your marks sync across the whole site.

    Can I see all 63 parks at once?

    Yes — with no filters applied, the explorer lists all 63 parks. Each park links to its detail page (tap the name on the main checklist or browse park pages) with highlights and planning info.

    Which parks are closest together for one trip?

    Utah's Mighty 5 (Arches, Bryce Canyon, Canyonlands, Capitol Reef, Zion) are the tightest cluster of iconic parks. Others: Florida's three, the Pacific Northwest three, and California's desert parks.

    Why do some parks appear under two states?

    A few parks cross state lines — Death Valley (California/Nevada), Great Smoky Mountains (Tennessee/North Carolina), and Yellowstone (Wyoming/Montana/Idaho). Filtering by either state will show them.

    Is the interactive map free?

    Yes — all filtering, searching, and marking is free with no account. Your visited marks are saved in your browser.

    Does this replace a paper map for road trips?

    No — bring a paper atlas as backup. This interactive explorer is best for planning at home; cell signal is unreliable inside many parks.
